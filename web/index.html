Why the south pole is so sensitive

The Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5° to the Sun, so at the pole the Sun circles the horizon instead of rising and setting. On Earth the midday Sun climbs up to 90°; here it never gets much above 2°. A ridge a few hundred metres high, tens of kilometres away, can hide it for days.

Earth stays in roughly one direction but bobs up and down by about ±7° every month as the Moon wobbles (libration). Near the pole that is enough to drop it below the skyline, cutting direct radio contact. On the far side it never rises at all.

There is no air to bend light, so the horizon is exactly the terrain plus the curve of the Moon. Moving a lander one kilometre, or landing a few days later, can change everything. Compare Apollo 11, where Earth hangs high in the sky all month.
